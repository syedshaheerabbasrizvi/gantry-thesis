\chapter{Drive Mechanisms}

The drive mechanism on each axis converts the rotary output of the servo motor into the linear or rotary motion required by the gantry. The selection of drive mechanism directly affects positioning accuracy, dynamic load capacity, speed, and system reliability. This chapter presents the requirements for each axis, evaluates candidate mechanisms against those requirements, and justifies the final selection.

\section{Drive Selection Requirements}

The key requirements governing the drive selection are derived from the system specifications in Table~\ref{tab:specs} and the motor sizing in Chapter~6:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Travel distance:} Up to \qty{4.0}{m} on the X and Y axes; up to \qty{2.0}{m} on the Z-axis.
    \item \textbf{Speed:} A peak linear velocity of approximately \qty{0.33}{m/s} (derived from the motion profile in Section~6.1) on all linear axes.
    \item \textbf{Payload:} The combined moving mass, including payload, is up to \qty{170}{kg} (with safety factor; see Table~6.1).
    \item \textbf{Anti-backdrive (Z-axis):} The vertical axis must not allow the payload to free-fall in the event of power loss. This is a safety-critical requirement.
    \item \textbf{Local availability and cost:} Components must be procurable through local suppliers within the project budget.
\end{itemize}

\section{X and Y Axes: Belt-and-Pulley Drive}

For the X and Y axes, the primary requirements are long travel (\qty{4.0}{m}), moderate speed, and low moving mass to maximise acceleration capability. Lead screws were eliminated immediately: at the required travel distance and speed, the rotational speed would exceed the critical speed of the screw, causing resonant whipping and potential catastrophic failure. Between the remaining candidates, the belt-and-pulley system was selected over rack-and-pinion for three reasons.

First, the belt drive allows the motor to remain stationary on the frame while the belt transmits motion along the axis. This significantly reduces the moving mass of the carriage, which in turn reduces the torque required for acceleration and permits a smaller, less expensive motor. In contrast, a rack-and-pinion system requires either the motor to travel with the carriage (adding to moving mass) or the rack to move (adding dead weight to the structure).

Second, belts are straightforward to install, tension, and replace, which supports the project's goal of long-term maintainability by Dawlance's engineering staff without specialised expertise.

Third, HTD\,8M synchronous timing belts --- selected for their availability in the local market and their rated load capacity exceeding the peak belt tension for these axes --- effectively eliminate slip through positive tooth engagement, addressing the primary concern with belt drives under heavy loads.

The trade-off is that belt drives are backdrivable, meaning the axes can move freely when unpowered. On the horizontal X and Y axes, this is acceptable because gravity does not act along the direction of travel; the electromagnetic brakes on the servo motors provide holding torque when the system is stationary.

The system uses a 20-tooth drive pulley on each axis, yielding a pitch diameter of $\frac{P \times Z}{\pi} = \frac{8 \times 20}{\pi} \approx \qty{50.9}{mm}$. A dual-motor configuration is used on the X-axis, with one motor on each side of the bridge, to provide symmetric actuation and prevent racking of the Y-axis bridge under load.

\section{Z-Axis: Rack-and-Pinion Drive}

The Z-axis has a fundamentally different requirement: it must support the suspended mass (payload, end-effector, and vertical structure) against gravity at all times, including during power loss. This makes the backdrive characteristic the decisive selection criterion.

Lead screws offer inherent backdrive resistance through thread friction, but were rejected for the Z-axis for the same reason as the horizontal axes: the travel distance of \qty{2.0}{m} at the required speed would approach the critical speed limit. Furthermore, precision ball screws --- which would resolve the speed issue --- are significantly more expensive and less available locally than standard rack-and-pinion components.

The rack-and-pinion mechanism was selected for the Z-axis because it provides the required rigidity and load capacity for vertical operation, and the backdrive concern is addressed by the electromagnetic brakes integrated into the Yaskawa Sigma-5 servo motors. When power is removed, the brakes engage automatically (they are spring-applied, electrically released), locking the Z-axis in place and preventing the payload from free-falling. This satisfies the safety requirement defined in Section~2.4.1.

A spur gear configuration (Module~2, 20~teeth, $20\degree$ pressure angle) was selected over helical gears because the straight teeth provide simpler engagement and alignment for the vertical drive, and do not introduce axial thrust loads that would need to be accommodated by the bearing arrangement. Two synchronised motors are used on the Z-axis, one driving each of two parallel racks, to distribute the load symmetrically and prevent binding.

Linear guide channels are incorporated along the Z-axis to bear lateral loads and maintain alignment during vertical traversal.

\section{Yaw Axis: Direct Drive}

The yaw axis rotates the end-effector to orient the microwave for placement. Unlike the linear axes, no transmission mechanism is required between the motor and the output; the motor is coupled directly to the yaw shaft through a 160:1 planetary drive reducer (discussed in Section~6.7). This high reduction ratio is necessary to manage the large rotational inertia of the payload and provide the orientation accuracy specified in Table~\ref{tab:specs}.

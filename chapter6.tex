\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}

\section{Summary of Work}

In this thesis, the risks of manual packaging and palletizing of microwaves at Dawlance's assembly plant were addressed; particularly, the immediate process redesign required by the calculated NIOSH Lifting Index of 3.3. The solution, which is a four-degree-of-freedom gantry robot operating under human--robot collaboration, performs the heavy lifting while workers retain the dexterous lightweight tasks.

Each subsystem has been designed, sized, and validated according to the constraints of the task: the mechanical frame, linear motion system, drive mechanisms, motor actuation and sizing, vacuum end-effector, STM32-based control, motion profiling, and computer vision for pose estimation. The final deliverable is a full-scale $4 \times 4 \times 2$\,m wooden prototype demonstrating pick-and-place on the translational axes with suction. Dawlance will receive mechanical design documentation and construct the final steel frame with the same components and architecture under supervisory guidance from the project team.

\section{Evaluation Against Objectives}

The six secondary objectives defined in Section~1.3 are evaluated against the metrics established in Section~2.5.

\subsection{Improve Ergonomics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric:} NIOSH Lifting Index (LI).
    \item \textbf{Target:} $\leq 1.0$, reduced from the current value of 3.3.
\end{itemize}

Since all lifting operations are offloaded to the gantry robot in this solution, the tasks the workers perform involve objects that weigh well under 2\,kg --- examples include inserting EPS blocks, sealing box flaps with tape, and wrapping the microwave in plastic --- without significant asymmetry or vertical displacement. With these factors effectively eliminated, the new Lifting Index is expected to fall well below 1.0; however, a formal reassessment is required for quantitative verification after the final solution is deployed at the plant.

\subsection{Maintain Product Quality}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric:} Product damage rate.
    \item \textbf{Target:} 0\% verified damage across operational trials.
\end{itemize}

The vacuum end-effector distributes the lifting force across six suction cups, so that the microwave may not be deformed at a single point. Moreover, the S-curve motion profiles used by the system ensure continuous acceleration and finite jerk, so that mechanical vibration can be suppressed during regular operation. And the computer vision pipeline aligns the poses of the objects before placement, so that the edges or the flaps may not collide inadvertently.

During testing, the vacuum gripper successfully lifted a plastic-wrapped Dawlance microwave shell without visible damage, and at the project showcase, the system continuously picked and moved the shell in the X and Y axes for over an hour with no grip failure. However, formal trials as defined in Section~2.5 remain to be performed, as they require the final steel structure and packaging materials. This is recommended to be part of the commissioning process.

\subsection{Meet Production Speed}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric:} System cycle time.
    \item \textbf{Target:} $\leq 30$\,s, with an optimisation target of 25\,s.
\end{itemize}

The motor sizing performed was against a motion profile requiring 3.0\,m of linear travel in 10\,s per axis, so that the remaining time of the 30\,s window could be utilised for gripping, releasing, and settling. SigmaSelect confirmed that all selected motors satisfy this profile with safety margin on torque, speed, and inertia ratio.

However, to avoid producing resonant vibrations on the wooden prototype, the axis movement was intentionally kept to low speeds, which means that the measured cycle times are not representative of production performance. While the trajectory planning and motor sizing in previous chapters theoretically confirm the achievement of the target, real-world validation under production conditions is also deferred to the commissioning phase once the final steel system is fabricated.

\subsection{Be Affordable}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric:} Final Bill of Materials cost.
    \item \textbf{Target:} $\leq$ Rs\,1.8\,million, with an optimisation target of Rs\,1.5\,million.
\end{itemize}

The estimated Bill of Materials --- detailed in Appendix~B --- amounts to approximately Rs\,1.8\,million, including a 25\% allocation for contingency purposes. While individual components evolved during designing and prototyping, the total remained within this approved budget. Moreover, this system is significantly less expensive than commercial solutions for similar tasks, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:cost_comparison}.

There are three primary reasons for this difference in cost. Firstly, the fabrication material of structural steel and aluminium extrusions is significantly less expensive than proprietary robot frames. Secondly, open-source control stacks such as the STM32 framework and OpenCV mean that no software is purchased. Thirdly, the gantry architecture permits standard linear motion components for each axis instead of the precision revolute joints and harmonic drives required at every axis of an articulated arm to support its own weight.

\subsection{Be Flexible}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric:} Number of Dawlance microwave models successfully handled without mechanical changeover.
    \item \textbf{Target:} Successful handling of all tested models with zero product damage.
\end{itemize}

To accommodate Dawlance's entire range of microwave models, the bellows-type suction cups conform to surface height variations across different models, and the computer vision pipeline estimates pose regardless of model dimensions, requiring no mechanical changeover. Additionally, the Grounding DINO + SAM2 pipeline (Section~\ref{sec:gdino_pipeline}) accepts text prompts and requires no retraining for new models. However, the prototype was tested with only one microwave model; formal multi-model trials are a commissioning milestone.

\subsection{Be Reliable}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Metric:} Gripper pick rate and place rate over 100 consecutive trials.
    \item \textbf{Target:} $\geq 99$ successful operations with zero product damage.
\end{itemize}

During the project showcase, the system performed continuous pick-and-move operations for over one hour without a single grip failure, providing preliminary confidence in reliability. However, the formal 100-cycle trial defined in Section~2.5 has not yet been conducted, as it requires the steel frame to operate at representative speeds and accelerations. Moreover, the yaw axis was not integrated into the wooden prototype due to insufficient frame rigidity to support the planetary drive under load. Yaw functionality has been validated with a hobby servomotor on the prototype and through component-level testing of the production motor, gearbox, and bearing assembly; its integration is planned for the steel structure.


\section{Limitations} \label{sec:limitations}

The following limitations were identified during the course of the project, beyond those anticipated in Section~2.4.2.

The wooden prototype, although full-scale in dimensions, could not replicate the stiffness of the intended steel frame because timber introduces resonances at the required traversal speeds. Due to this, cycle time and dynamic performance data cannot be directly extrapolated at this stage.

The Grounding DINO and SAM2 variant of the computer vision pipeline achieves a position error of approximately 5\% relative to the object's distance from the camera origin. At working distances of 1--2\,m, this leads to errors of 5--10\,cm, which exceeds the $<1$\,cm positional accuracy specified in Table~\ref{tab:specs}. This is believed to be attributable to camera calibration and lighting conditions rather than a fundamental limitation of the detection and segmentation pipeline, and is expected to improve with more rigorous calibration. Additionally, the pipeline has not yet been tested with the full range of Dawlance microwave models, some of which have different surface finishes and colours, nor under the variable lighting conditions of the factory floor.

Vacuum leakage at pneumatic interfaces is a practical challenge. While the suction cups themselves seal reliably on microwave surfaces, the threaded connections between cups, T-connectors, and tubing were observed to produce minor leaks that reduce the effective vacuum level. Production deployment will need professional pneumatic fitting with proper sealing at all joints.

The yaw axis was not integrated into the prototype. While the mechanical design and motor sizing have been validated through CAD analysis and SigmaSelect, physical integration and testing remain pending.

The supervisory interface is currently a command-line tool suitable for development and testing. A graphical operator interface would be necessary for deployment on the factory floor, where line operators are not expected to interact with a terminal. Moreover, the high-level control architecture --- including the ROS2 node structure outlined in Chapter~\ref{ch:interface} --- is yet to be implemented, which requires integration of the vision pipeline with automated sequencing.


\section{Future Work} 

\subsection{Near-Term: Deployment Path} \label{sec:near_term}

The immediate priority is to fabricate the steel structure using the mechanical design documentation provided as part of the project handover with Dawlance's support. This includes I-beam framing, precision-machined mounting plates for the linear guide rails, and motor housings. Following fabrication, the electrical and control subsystems (which have been validated on the wooden prototype) can be transferred directly to the steel frame.

Once the steel structure is assembled, the following commissioning steps are recommended: integration and testing of the yaw axis; execution of the 100-cycle pick/place trials and 40-hour operational trial defined in Section~2.5; a formal NIOSH Lifting Index reassessment of the redesigned manual task profile; and development of an operator HMI to replace the command-line interface.

\subsection{Longer-Term: Extensions}

Several directions merit further development beyond the initial deployment.

The computer vision pipeline can be extended to detect human workers within the gantry workspace, enabling active safety monitoring in compliance with ISO/TS~15066 speed and separation monitoring requirements. This would strengthen the human--robot collaboration capabilities of the system.

The open-source control stack is not specific to microwave packaging. The same architecture could be adapted for other packaging lines at Dawlance (e.g., washing machines, refrigerators) or at other Pakistani manufacturers, providing a replicable blueprint for affordable industrial automation. More broadly, the modular software architecture could be generalised for other pick-and-place applications, similar to open-source platforms such as OpenPnP, but scaled for heavy industrial payloads.

Finally, the end-effector control can be enhanced by fusing vacuum pressure sensor data with motor current feedback to implement compliant placement, allowing gentler insertion of the microwave into its box and reducing the risk of edge strikes. This would build on industrial literature where compliant manipulation typically relies on expensive six-axis force-torque sensors.


\section{Closing Remarks}

This project demonstrates that industrial automation for heavy-payload packaging and palletizing need not depend on imported proprietary systems or require capital expenditure beyond the reach of manufacturers in developing economies. The proposed gantry system, at a total cost of approximately Rs\,1.8\,million (\textasciitilde\$6,000 USD), delivers comparable payload and workspace specifications to commercial palletising cells costing \$80,000--\$120,000, while using an entirely open-source control stack and locally sourced structural components. The human--robot collaboration approach preserves employment for line workers by offloading only the ergonomically hazardous tasks, aligning with the principles of Industry~5.0 and the broader goal of sustainable, human-centric manufacturing.

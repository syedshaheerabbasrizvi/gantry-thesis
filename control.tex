\chapter{Low-Level Control}

\section{Controller Selection}

The low-level controller is responsible for generating precisely timed pulse trains to command the servo drives, parsing commands from the high-level PC, implementing motion profiles, and handling safety-critical interrupts such as limit switches and emergency stops. Two classes of controller were evaluated: embedded microcontrollers and industrial programmable logic controllers (PLCs).

\subsection{Microcontroller vs.\ PLC}

Industrial automation conventionally uses PLCs for multi-axis servo control. PLCs capable of coordinated multi-axis pulse output, such as the Siemens S7-1500 or Allen-Bradley ControlLogix, provide deterministic scan cycles, built-in motion libraries, and IEC~61131-3 programming environments. However, these units cost Rs\,300,000--500,000 for a configuration supporting six axes with high-speed pulse outputs, which represents a significant fraction of the total project budget of Rs\,1.8\,million. The STM32F303 microcontroller provides equivalent pulse-generation capability through hardware timer peripherals at a cost under Rs\,5,000, with the trade-off of requiring custom firmware development rather than using pre-built motion function blocks.

Given the project's emphasis on affordability and open-source control (Section~2.6), the microcontroller approach was selected. The Arduino Uno (ATmega328P) was used during early prototyping for rapid validation, before transitioning to the STM32F303 Discovery Kit for final deployment. The STM32F303 was selected for final deployment for the following reasons. Its 32-bit native integer width eliminates overflow risk when computing step counts that routinely exceed the 16-bit limit of 65,535. The hardware FPU computes the trigonometric functions required for S-curve profiles in a fraction of the time required by software emulation on the ATmega328P, ensuring consistent inter-pulse timing. The NVIC allows the limit switch interrupt to be assigned the highest priority, guaranteeing immediate response regardless of what other task the CPU is executing. The DMA controller allows UART reception of JSON commands from the PC into a RAM buffer without CPU involvement, eliminating the blocking serial communication that caused timing jitter on the Arduino. Finally, hardware timer Output Compare mode can generate the pulse train entirely in hardware, freeing the CPU for other tasks during the cruise region of the motion profile.

\section{Drive Interfacing}

The Yaskawa Sigma-5 SGDV servo drives (introduced in Chapter~6 as the selected drive for all axes) are commanded using a pulse/direction protocol, analogous to stepper motor control. Each rising edge on the PULS pin increments an internal position counter on the drive, or decrements it if SIGN is LOW. The drive's internal PID controller then generates the motor torque required to move the shaft to the commanded position, using encoder feedback for closed-loop correction.

\subsection{Electronic Gear Ratio}

The motor encoder has a 20-bit resolution of $2^{20} = 1{,}048{,}576$ counts per revolution, which is inconvenient for firmware arithmetic. The drive's electronic gear ratio feature maps this to a user-defined pulse count: register Pn20E is set to 1,048,576 and Pn210 to 10,000, so that one full revolution corresponds to exactly 10,000 command pulses. Each pulse therefore commands approximately 104.86 encoder counts of shaft displacement. This was experimentally verified on the test bench by sending exactly 10,000 pulses and confirming a single revolution both visually on the motor shaft and through the pulse count displayed on the drive.

\subsection{Signal Topology: Open-Collector vs.\ Differential}

During prototyping, an open-collector topology was employed, where the MCU GPIO drives current through a resistor into the CN1 optocoupler. This topology is single-ended: only one wire carries the signal, measured relative to ground. While functional on the bench with short cable runs, this approach is unsuitable for deployment on the full-scale gantry for two reasons. First, electromagnetic interference (EMI) from the high-frequency PWM switching in the motor power cables can corrupt the single-ended signal, and the noise amplitude grows with cable length and pulse rate. Second, the open-collector output is passive --- it can only pull the line low, leading to weak drive strength and susceptibility to parasitic capacitance on long runs.

To address both concerns, the final design implements a differential line driver (AM26LS31) for the PULS and SIGN signals. In this topology, two wires carry inverted copies of the same signal. Common-mode noise (such as EMI) affects both wires equally and is rejected by the differential receiver at the drive input. For effective common-mode rejection, the two conductors must experience the same electromagnetic environment, which is ensured by using shielded twisted pair (STP) cabling.

The differential topology also provides active drive in both directions (push-pull), ensuring sharp signal edges regardless of cable length and parasitic load.

\subsection{Pulse Mode Selection}

The SGDV supports three input pulse modes within the differential topology:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sign + pulse:} One pair sends a pulse train for position increments; the other pair sends a static HIGH or LOW for direction. Simple to generate and debug, but requires settling time when changing direction.
    \item \textbf{CW + CCW:} Separate pulse pairs for forward and reverse motion. No settling time for direction changes, but has undefined behaviour if both pairs are active simultaneously.
    \item \textbf{Quadrature (2-phase):} Both pairs send signals 90$\degree$ out of phase; direction is determined by which pair leads. Quadruples effective resolution by counting both rising and falling edges on both pairs. However, this is non-trivial to generate in firmware and is typically used with proprietary motion controllers.
\end{enumerate}

Sign + pulse mode was selected, as the required pulse rate is relatively low (under 10\,kHz at maximum traversal speed) and the shielded twisted pair cabling provides adequate noise immunity without the complexity of quadrature generation.

\section{Command Trajectory}

The SGDV's internal PID controller corrects position error by modulating motor torque. However, it cannot smooth the incoming command stream: if a rapid burst of pulses arrives, the drive sees an instantaneous position error and demands maximum torque, causing mechanical shock, vibration, and potentially the A.d00 following error fault. A motion profile is therefore required at the command level, upstream of the drive, to ensure that the pulse rate ramps smoothly.

\subsection{S-Curve Profile}

A trapezoidal velocity profile --- where velocity ramps linearly during acceleration and deceleration --- is an improvement over a step input, but produces discontinuous acceleration at the ramp corners, resulting in theoretically infinite jerk. For the gantry, where belt compliance and frame flexibility amplify jerk-induced vibrations, this is insufficient.

The S-curve profile addresses this by ramping the acceleration itself, so that the velocity transitions follow a smooth S-shape and jerk remains finite throughout the motion. The STM32 firmware approximates the S-curve using a sinusoidal ease function:

\begin{equation}
    t = \frac{i}{N_\text{accel}}, \qquad \tilde{t} = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - \cos(\pi t)\right)
\end{equation}

where $i$ is the current step index within the acceleration region and $N_\text{accel}$ is the total number of acceleration steps --- that is, the number of motor pulses over which the inter-pulse delay transitions from $d_\text{max}$ (slowest speed) to $d_\text{min}$ (fastest speed). The normalised variable $t$ represents linear progress through the ramp, and the shaped variable $\tilde{t}$ maps this to a smooth curve whose derivative is zero at both endpoints, ensuring zero jerk at the transitions into and out of the acceleration region.

The inter-pulse delay is then interpolated:

\begin{equation}
    d(i) = d_\text{min} + (d_\text{max} - d_\text{min})(1 - \tilde{t})
\end{equation}

Since delay is inversely proportional to velocity (shorter delay between pulses means faster motion), $\tilde{t} = 0$ corresponds to minimum speed and $\tilde{t} = 1$ to maximum speed.

\subsection{Profile Regions}

The complete motion profile consists of three regions: acceleration, cruise, and deceleration. The deceleration region mirrors the acceleration region by indexing from the end of the move rather than the beginning. This symmetry imposes a geometric constraint: the acceleration region cannot exceed half the total step count, because beyond that point it would overlap with the deceleration region. Hence:

\[
N_\text{accel} = \min\left(N_\text{accel,\,desired},\; \frac{N_\text{total}}{2}\right)
\]

If the total move is short enough that the acceleration and deceleration regions consume all available steps, no cruise region exists and the motion consists of a symmetric ramp up and down. During the cruise region, the delay is constant at $d_\text{min}$ and the velocity is flat at its peak value.

\subsection{Multi-Axis Coordination}

For simultaneous multi-axis movement, all axes must start and stop together to produce an approximately linear path of the end-effector. This is achieved by synchronising all axes to the delay curve of the dominant axis --- the axis with the greatest step count. If the X-axis requires 1,000 steps and the Y-axis requires 500 steps, both axes execute 1,000 delay iterations, but the Y-axis only issues a pulse on every second iteration. This pulse-skipping ensures that all axes complete their respective distances simultaneously, without requiring each axis to have its own independent timing source.

The S-curve firmware has been implemented and validated on the Arduino prototype (Figure~\ref{fig:motor_bench}) and is being migrated to the STM32, where the hardware FPU computes the sinusoidal ease function more efficiently.

\section{Homing Routine}

A homing routine establishes a known coordinate reference for each axis at power-on. Without homing, the servo drives maintain only relative position from the point at which they were last energised, and any accumulated drift or physical disturbance during power-off would propagate as a systematic offset in all subsequent moves.

The homing procedure, implemented on the X and Y axes of the prototype, operates in two phases. In the first phase, the axis moves toward the limit switch at moderate speed until the switch triggers, at which point the axis halts immediately via the NVIC high-priority interrupt. In the second phase, the axis reverses at low speed until the switch releases. The position at which the switch releases is registered as the axis origin (home position), and all subsequent motion commands are referenced to this coordinate.

Homing is performed once at power-on. During normal operation, the servo drives' internal 20-bit encoders maintain absolute position; re-homing is only required after a fault recovery, an emergency stop event, or a physical disturbance that may have displaced the carriage while the drives were de-energised.

\chapter{Design Details}

\label{sec:design_details}

This section details the technical approach to solving the design problem. This includes the system architecture, the alternatives considered, the results from proof-of-concept experimentation, and a deeper dive into the core subsystems.

\section{Solution Statement}

The solution is a single 4-axis gantry robot operating under Industry 5.0 philosophy, which handles the heavy lifting of the process, while offloading the dexterous tasks to line workers. Its X and Y is belt-driven, Z-axis is rack-and-pinion driven, and yaw axis is directly driven, all by AC servo motors. The end-effector is purely vacuum based, with suction cups and pressure feedback, and tracking of microwaves and boxes is through computer vision. This system is fully locally manufactured and maintainable without foreign expertise, with all components procured locally to eliminate dependence on foreign supply chains.

The solution is informed by the constraints, as discussed in the literature review. Maintaining a fully manual process was not feasible because of the NIOSH Lifting Index being 3.1, which requires immediate redesign. Due to the conservative cycle time, full automation was rejected, particularly for dexterous tasks. Out of the two options (owing to the heavy payload), articulated robots were rejected due to concerns of budget and maintainability, leaving gantry robots as the only sensible option. Lighter alternatives were disqualified by the 40 kg payload requirement. Integrating the arguments together, the project is left to be a gantry robot under human-robot collaboration, as that is the only architecture satisfying the major constraints simultaneously.

The primary ethical driver of the project is to address occupational safety in the factory; however, to retain the employment of line workers, the approach adopts human-robot collaboration. To promote sustainability, the project utilizes motors with the minimum sufficient power ratings and S-curve motion profiles to minimize energy usage. The automated handling of microwaves also targets waste reduction, as fewer microwaves are discarded due to manual lifting errors stemming from fatigue.

The figure demonstrates how the hardware integrates throughout the solution. An industrial PC takes in data from the camera on the gantry robot, and develops motion sequencing algorithms based on computer vision, while presenting a command-line interface (CLI) in Python for users. It also acts as a supervisory state machine to transition between the logic states using ROS2.

This forwards position data to the STM32F303 Discovery Kit, which acts as a low-level controller. It parses commands, sequences motion, and generates S-curve profiles to conserve energy while ensuring that acceleration and jerk are continuous to suppress vibration in the motors. Moreover, it incorporates homing and safety logic through wiring with the drives, so that in case of an alarm, it can act as an emergency stop in software alongside a physical button on the gantry robot.

These forward the trajectory to the AC servo drives, which perform position control for each respective motor. Since the motors come with their own encoders and cabling, the drives internally perform PID control to minimize errors. 

Then, the X and Y motors transmit their torque onto belt-and-pulley systems, while the Z motor transmits to a rack-and-pinion system. Meanwhile, the yaw motor is directly driving the end effector, which consists of suction cups with a vacuum generator connected as well as a solenoid valve for pneumatic switching.

To power all the hardware, the system has a power electronics panel, which takes in 400V 3-phase AC power from the factory, and incorporates safety measures, such as a circuit-breaker, and powers the drives and motors. It also produces a single-phase 230V AC supply for the drive's control hardware, as well as a 24V DC rail for the electromagnetic brakes on the brakes, the pressure sensor, the external encoders, and the solenoid for the end-effector. Finally, it also produces logic rails of 5V and 3.3V for powering the STM32 and its logic circuitry simultaneously. 

Finally, the loop is completed using a sensing system. The main component is the global shutter camera, which provides an image stream to the high-level controller using a USB connection. The output shafts have external encoders for confirmation of axes movement, while the axes themselves have limits for homing and detecting corners. And the vacuum system on the end-effector has a pressure sensor so that the grip on the object is sufficient.

\section{System Details}

While the block diagram demonstrates how the hardware is set up -- particularly how data, power, and mechanical translation flows across the system -- the logical subsystems manifest slightly differently. For example, the domain of the camera dwarfs that of the remaining sensors, and warrants an entire chapter. The mechanical structure is what multiple parts of the robot rely on, but are passive in terms of data flow, and cannot be represented easily in the diagram. 

Hence, the project is partitioned into the following subsystems.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mechanical Structure:} This supports the entire robot. The frame contains two parallel X-axis beams, a Y-axis bridge spanning between them, a Z-axis mast hanging from the carriage, and an assembly incorporate both the yaw and the end-effector at the bottom. Linear bearings and guides are also incorporated to bear the weight without significant friction of the axes. 
    \item \textbf{Drive Mechanisms:} The X and Y-axes employ belt \& pulley systems driven by the AC servo motors, while the Z-axis is driven by a spur rack \& pinion mechanism. Meanwhile, the yaw axis is directly driven through a high-ratio gearbox.
    \item \textbf{Motor Actuation:} The system utilizes Yaskawa Sigma-5 AC Servos for closed-loop position control. Each of these are operated with their own drive and are paired with gearboxes to prioritize handling the high weight and inertia of the payload while balancing speed requirements.
    \item \textbf{Power Electronics:} The electrical subsystem utilizes a 400V 3-phase AC input from the factory, and partitions power into multiple stages: a 400V 3-phase line for drive and motor power, a single-phase AC line for the logic controllers of the drives, a 24V DC rail for electromagnetic brakes and pneumatic solenoids, and 5V/3.3V DC rails for powering the STM32 and its logic circuitry. 
    \item \textbf{End-Effector:} The end-effector uses a vacuum-based lifting mechanism for lifting microwave shells and boxes, even if coated with a plastic bag. This includes a suction cup, a vacuum pump, and solenoid-controlled switching, though the final deployment will expand to six cups (two large, four small) for distributing lifting force. The yaw mechanism also interacts directly with this assembly, consisting of an upside-down motor coupled via a shaft and angular bearings for full rotational motion. 
    \item \textbf{Low-Level Control:} The STM32F303 Discovery Kit contains the firmware to interface with the Yaskawa drives with a pulse/direction protocol implemented using differential line to mitigate electromagnetic interference. The STM32 also implements S-curve motion profiles to minimize mechanical jerk, and contains a homing routine utilize limit switches on the XYZ-axes for calibration purposes.
    \item \textbf{Computer Vision:} The vision subsystem uses an AR0234 Global Shutter camera mounted at an angle on the gantry frame, with a pipeline hinging on classical Open CV -- particularly contour detection and thresholding -- to identify 2D bounding boxes of the objects on the line. This provides the X and Y coordinates needed for alignment.
    \item \textbf{High-Level Control:} This supervisory layer runs on industrial PC -- or a laptop for the prototype -- which communicates with the STM32 via UART over USB. It acts as a command-line interface for teleoperation, distance conversion, and tuning parameters as required. 
\end{itemize}

Each of the subsystems is explained in the following chapters.

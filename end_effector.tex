\chapter{End Effector Design}
\label{sec:ee_design}

The end effector (EE) is the most mechanically critical subsystem of the gantry, 
as all design decisions are fundamentally constrained by the properties of the 
target objects: the microwave shell, its corrugated cardboard packaging, and the 
protective plastic wrapping. Three primary architectural approaches were evaluated 
before arriving at a final design.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Purely Mechanical:} Side-gripping or form-closure fingers 
        applying normal force to achieve frictional lift.
    \item \textbf{Purely Vacuum:} A suction cup array maintained under continuous 
        evacuation, relying on atmospheric pressure differential for grip.
    \item \textbf{Hybrid:} A combination of the above, where the vacuum array 
        provides primary adhesion and mechanical fingers provide lateral retention 
        during high-speed movement.
\end{itemize}

\section{Architectural Selection}

\subsection{Mechanical Constraints}

The feasibility of the mechanical approach is governed by the material 
properties of Dawlance's packaging. The corrugated cardboard is a 5-layer 
B+B construction with 3\,mm flute size. For static equilibrium, the friction force must equal the weight of the payload:
$F_f = mg = \mu \cdot F_n$, hence:

$$ F_f = \mu \cdot F_n \implies (40\,\text{kg} \times 10\,\text{N/kg}) 
= 0.4 \cdot F_n \implies F_n = 1000\,\text{N} $$

where $\mu = 0.4$ is the estimated coefficient of friction between the gripper 
pads and the cardboard surface \cite{rudawska2024}. However, structural testing 
(edge crushing, box crushing, and face crushing tests) indicates that the 
cardboard staple joints fail at approximately 200\,N \cite{singh2009}, 
making force-closure mechanical gripping mechanically infeasible without 
damaging the packaging.

The maximum safe gripping force is therefore limited to 370\,N, applying a 
1.5$\times$ safety factor to the 550\,N joint failure load. For completeness, 
the microwave shell itself (1\,mm bent aluminium, yield strength 140\,MPa) 
imposes a separate force limit.

\subsection{Vacuum Feasibility}

The vacuum system was validated experimentally by lifting microwaves both 
bare and through plastic wrapping, with loads verified up to 40\,kg using 
calibrated weights. A key concern was horizontal slippage under the 
accelerations demanded by the 30\,s cycle time. With a 10\,s traversal 
window, the peak acceleration is 0.5\,m/s$^2$, yielding a horizontal 
shear force of:

$$ F_{\text{shear}} = m \cdot a = 40\,\text{kg} \times 0.5\,\text{m/s}^2 
= 20\,\text{N} $$

By centering the payload mass between the primary suction cups, this load 
distributes to 10\,N per contact point, which falls well within the rated 
shear envelope of the selected Festo grippers. The hybrid approach was 
therefore also rejected: since slippage was not observed experimentally, 
the additional mechanical complexity of the hybrid system offered no 
measurable benefit.

\section{Suction Cup Selection}

With the vacuum architecture confirmed, the specific suction cup geometry 
was evaluated. The primary challenge is surface non-uniformity: microwaves 
have lips, ridges, and height steps at the shell boundary, while cardboard 
boxes have flap seams. Flat rigid cups require near-perfect surface contact 
and are therefore unsuitable. Table~\ref{tab:suction_cup_types} compares 
the four candidate geometries.

Bellows cups were selected for their compliance on non-uniform surfaces and 
confirmed ability to grip through plastic wrapping. Although their larger 
internal volume (120\,cm$^3$) increases evacuation time relative to flat 
cups, the 200\,ms engagement time remains compatible with the 400\,ms 
gripping window, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:vacuum_calc}. Multi-stage 
cups were unavailable locally, and foam-backed cups, while compliant, 
introduce sealing variability that is difficult to model reliably, and are incredibly expensive. 

\subsection{Array Configuration}

The array consists of two large central suction cups (Festo 125\,mm bellows) 
and four peripheral smaller cups (Festo 50\,mm bellows). This arrangement 
distributes the lifting force evenly across the microwave surface, preventing 
shell deformation, while the peripheral cups provide moment resistance against 
tipping during lateral acceleration.

The high-suction venturi was selected. The system does not require deep 
vacuum: 0.5\,bar absolute is sufficient to achieve the required lift 
capacity, as confirmed in the calculations below. The high-suction 
variant's 80--95\,L/min flow rate is the critical advantage, enabling 
fast evacuation of the bellows cup array within the 400\,ms gripping 
window. The high-vacuum variant, while capable of deeper evacuation, 
provides insufficient flow rate to evacuate the system volume in time.

\subsection{Vacuum Calculations}

The total evacuated volume is computed from the cup and piping geometry. 
All six suction cups share a common vacuum manifold, so the full array 
volume must be evacuated simultaneously:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Large Suction Cups:} $2 \times 125$\,mm bellows at 
        307\,cm$^3$ each $= 614$\,cm$^3$
    \item \textbf{Small Suction Cups:} $4 \times 50$\,mm bellows at 
        approximately 32\,cm$^3$ each $= 128$\,cm$^3$
    \item \textbf{Piping:} 8\,mm diameter tubing, 3\,m total length:
        $$ V_{\text{pipe}} = \pi r^2 L = \pi (0.4\,\text{cm})^2 
        \times 300\,\text{cm} \approx 150.8\,\text{cm}^3 $$
    \item \textbf{Total:} $V_{\text{total}} \approx 892.8$\,cm$^3$ 
        (0.893\,L)
\end{itemize}

The required flow rate $Q$ to achieve 0.5\,bar absolute within the 
400\,ms gripping window is:

$$ Q = \frac{V_{\text{total}} \cdot \ln(P_{\text{atm}}/P_{\text{final}})}{t} 
= \frac{0.893 \cdot \ln(1/0.5)}{0.4/60} \approx 92.8\,\text{L/min} $$

The selected venturi generator (Festo VN-14) is rated at 92.6\,L/min at 
5\,bar supply pressure. This is marginally below the theoretical requirement; 
however, the calculation assumes worst-case simultaneous evacuation from 
atmospheric pressure. In practice, the residual vacuum from the previous 
cycle reduces the effective volume to be evacuated, and the 400\,ms window 
includes conservative margin. Experimental testing confirmed reliable 
engagement within this window.

\section{Yaw Axis Design}

The yaw axis orients the payload prior to placement. The largest production unit 
at Dawlance, modelled as a rotating table with its physical dimensions, yields a 
moment of inertia of approximately 2.12\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$, which demanded both a 
high gear reduction (160:1 planetary drive, as sized in Section~6.7) and careful 
shaft design to minimise reflected inertia while remaining manufacturable with 
local machining capabilities.

\subsection{Shaft Design}

Three shaft geometries were considered, as compared in Table~\ref{tab:yaw_shaft_design}. A pure solid shaft was rejected because it provides no shoulder geometry to seat the bearings against, making reliable axial location impossible. A complex multi-step shaft was rejected because the tight tolerances it requires (grinding-level precision on each step) exceed what is available at local machine shops in Karachi. A simple stepped shaft, with a single abutment step on an otherwise solid 30\,mm bar, was selected as it provides the necessary bearing seat while remaining achievable on a basic lathe with tolerances of $\pm$0.5\,mm.

\subsection{Bearing Retention}

With the shaft geometry fixed, the method of axially retaining the two 
angular contact bearings was evaluated. The key requirement is 
differential retention: the top bearing must be rigidly preloaded to 
carry the primary axial and radial loads, while the bottom bearing must 
be free to float axially, allowing it to self-align and absorb any shaft 
runout or dimensional variation in the housing seats. 
Table~\ref{tab:yaw_retention} compares the four candidate retention 
methods. Two locknuts on the top bearing provide controlled, adjustable 
preload. A single circlip on the bottom bearing permits the required 
axial float while preventing gross displacement.

\subsection{Bearing Configuration}

Angular contact ball bearings were selected over tapered roller and 
combined radial-axial alternatives due to their availability in Pakistan, 
lower cost, and adequate load capacity for the 40\,kg payload. The 
mounting configuration --- back-to-back (DB) versus face-to-face (DF) 
--- was then evaluated, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:yaw_bearing_config}. 
The back-to-back arrangement was selected for its superior radial and 
axial stiffness (165 and 185\,N/$\mu$m respectively), which directly 
benefits servo tuning stability and positional accuracy during yaw 
rotation under load.

The final yaw axis assembly therefore comprises: a 30\,mm simple stepped 
solid shaft, two angular contact ball bearings in a back-to-back 
configuration, two locknuts retaining the top (fixed) bearing, and a 
single circlip retaining the bottom (floating) bearing. The motor is 
mounted laterally and coupled through the 160:1 planetary drive reducer.

\subsection{Structural Framework}

The EE frame consists of aluminium 40/40 extrusions arranged in a 
quadrilateral configuration. The suction cup array is mounted to a main 
board via a sandwiched plate, with custom brackets secured to the 
extrusions using T-nuts.

\section{Summary}

The final end effector design integrates a bellows suction cup array 
(two 125\,mm and four 50\,mm Festo cups) driven by a high-suction 
venturi generator at 0.5\,bar absolute, mounted on a 30\,mm simple 
stepped shaft with back-to-back angular contact bearings and asymmetric 
locknut-circlip retention. At 40\,kPa vacuum, the theoretical lift 
capacity is 123\,kg. Applying a 65\% efficiency factor for semi-porous 
surfaces, the effective safe lift is approximately 80\,kg --- a safety 
factor of 2.0 on the 40\,kg design payload.
